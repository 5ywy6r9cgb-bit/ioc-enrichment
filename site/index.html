    How to File
    
        
    Ohio public records in six steps
    
        
    Ohio Revised Code § 149.43. You don't have to give your name or a reason, and you can't be made to put the request in writing.
    
      
    
    
    
    
      
      
    1. Find the office that holds it
      Records belong to the office that created or keeps them. Water billing is the utility, parcel data is the county auditor, deeds are the recorder, and dockets are the clerk of courts. Use the Atlas.
      2. 
      
    2. Ask for records, not answers
      An office has to give you existing records. It doesn't have to answer questions or create new documents. "Copies of the contract" works. "Why did you sign it?" doesn't.
      3. 
      
    3. Be specific and keep it reasonable
      Name the subject, the record type, and the time period. Overly broad requests can be denied, but the office then has to help you narrow them (R.C. 149.43(B)(2)).
      4. 
      
    4. Keep private data out of it
      Ask for aggregate totals and policies, not individual people's accounts. It's faster, easier to approve, and nobody's privacy gets caught in your story.
      5. 
      
    5. Follow up on a schedule
      Ohio sets no fixed number of days. Inspection is "promptly" and copies come "within a reasonable period of time." The reminders in My Requests are your follow-up schedule, not legal deadlines, so never tell an office it "missed the statutory deadline."
      6. 
      
    6. If they deny it, get the reason in writing
      A denial has to explain the legal authority it relies on (R.C. 149.43(B)(3)). Then you can file a complaint with the Ohio Court of Claims ($25, R.C. 2743.75) or bring a mandamus action.
      7. 
    
    
    
    
      Primary sources. Quote the statute from codes.ohio.gov, never from a summary. The Attorney General's Sunshine Laws manual is the standard reference.
