# Launch plan — building a reputation, post by post

A reputation for accuracy is the only asset this newsroom has. It is built the
slow way and lost in one post. So the sequence below is deliberately backwards
from what feels natural: **the least interesting material goes first**, because
credibility has to exist before an accusation can land.

---

## The one strategic decision

**Do not open with your own case.**

It is the strongest story you have and the worst one to launch on. A brand-new
account whose first post is "this happened to me" reads as a grievance, and
every viewer files it that way before hearing a fact. The same story, posted by
an account with a three-month record of sourced public-records work, reads as
an investigation.

Same facts. Completely different reception. The only variable is what came
first.

There is a legal reason too, and your own chronology already states it: the
certified dismissal entry is not in custody, and counsel should see the
material before publication, not after. That is not an obstacle to the plan —
it is the reason the plan starts elsewhere.

---

## Phase 0 — before you post anything

- [ ] Obtain the certified dismissal entry from the Franklin County Clerk
- [ ] Consult a §1983 attorney with the chronology and the document set
- [ ] Secure handles on every platform you might use, even ones you won't
- [ ] Decide the posting name now and never change it
- [ ] Shoot the standing kit from `SHOTLIST.md` — one Saturday, done forever
- [ ] Write your correction policy, publicly, before you need it

That last item is the highest-leverage thing on the list. See Phase 4.

---

## Phase 1 — Establish the method (weeks 1–3)

You are answering one question: *why should anyone believe this account?*

**Post 1 — Episode 00.** The video you already have. It makes no claim about
any person. It says: here is the standard, here is what the machine refuses to
do, audit us.

**Post 2 — a still from the hash scene.** One image, one line:
> "Every video we publish ships with a manifest — the SHA-256 of every file
> that produced it. If we claim it, the build enforced it."

**Post 3 — the refusal list.** A carousel or a single card of the nine things
the system refuses to do. This is the most shareable thing you own in the first
month, because it is unusual and verifiable and about nobody.

**Post 4 — "how to file a public records request in Ohio."** Plain, useful,
free. No story attached.

That fourth post is the growth engine and the reason to do it early. Teaching
the method recruits people who will use it, and someone who has filed a request
because you showed them how becomes an audience that understands why your
sourcing matters. Utility travels further than outrage and it decays slower.

---

## Phase 2 — Prove it on someone else's story (weeks 4–8)

The Dublin drone material. Public records, no personal stake, and it
demonstrates the method on a story where nobody can say you are the aggrieved
party.

Run it as a series, one document per post, not one big drop:

**Post 5 — the number.** $328,000 paid, zero cameras owned. Cite the purchase
order on the card.

**Post 6 — the terms.** The invoice line: payment for two years up front,
refunds not accepted. A `quote` scene with the crop.

**Post 7 — the blank line.** The contract checklist with the legislative
approval line left empty. Show the document.

**Post 8 — what the record does NOT show.** The records response saying there
is nothing responsive about federal data sharing. An absence, stated as an
absence, is one of the most honest posts you can make and almost nobody makes
it.

**Post 9 — the placement.** Four docking stations, two at schools.

**Post 10 — what changed.** HB 251 made drone flight records public. End with
the ask: request your own city's flight logs, here's the template.

Then Episode 01 — the full cut, assembled from the same material, posted last.
The short posts are the trailer; the episode is the payoff for people who
followed the thread.

---

## Phase 3 — Make it a beat, not a burst (weeks 9 onward)

Two recurring formats, alternating, roughly twice a week:

**"We asked. Here's what came back."** One request, one response, one document.
Even a denial is a post — especially a denial. The clock running is content.

**"Here's how you'd check this yourself."** Take a claim in the news and show
the record that settles it. You are not breaking the story; you are showing the
method on live material, which is what makes people trust you with a story you
*do* break.

Consistency matters more than volume here. Twice a week for six months beats
daily for three weeks and then silence, because the thing you are building is
the expectation that you will still be there.

---

## Phase 4 — The correction post

Publish your correction policy in week 1, before you need it. Then, the first
time you get something wrong — and you will — correct it loudly, at the top,
with what was wrong and what the record actually says.

This is counterintuitive and it is the single strongest reputational move
available to you. Everyone claims accuracy. Almost nobody demonstrates the
mechanism that catches their own errors. A visible correction proves the
machine works in the only way a claim never can.

Your system already produced two of these in development: an on-screen check
count that went stale within an hour, and six narration lines flagged as
unsayable in the time available. That is not embarrassing. **That is the
product.** A newsroom that catches its own drift is the entire pitch.

---

## Phase 5 — Your own case (only after counsel clears it)

By now you have three months of sourced work and an audience that knows how you
handle documents. Post it as a chronology, exactly as `CHRONOLOGY.md` is
written: dated rows, each citing the document that proves it, no adjectives.

Publish the chronology version, not the argument version. The argument belongs
in a filing drafted by counsel, where it is privileged and can be pleaded
properly. In a video, every characterization becomes a prior statement
available at deposition — and the flat timeline is more damaging to a bad case
anyway, because it cannot be argued with.

## What to measure

Not followers. Watch these instead:

- **saves and shares** over likes — saves mean useful, likes mean agreeable
- **completion rate** on the vertical cuts — if it drops at a scene, that scene
  is too long; change `dur` and re-render
- **how many people tell you they filed a request** — the only number that
  means the method spread
- **whether anyone has had to correct you** — if they have, and you published
  the correction, that is a win

---

## What will actually kill this

In order of likelihood:

1. **Posting the personal case first.** Frames everything after it as grievance.
2. **One unsourced claim.** Undoes every sourced one. The gate exists for this.
3. **A characterization you cannot support.** "Illegal," "they knew," "false
   charges" — argument dressed as reporting, and quotable against you later.
4. **Publishing a face or an address by accident.** Crop before it enters
   `assets/`, never after.
5. **Going quiet for six weeks.** Slower than the others, just as fatal.
6. **Borrowed music or images.** The one asset with no provenance, on a channel
   whose whole claim is provenance. This is why the score is generated.

## The cadence, compressed

| week | post |
|---|---|
| 1 | Episode 00 · the manifest still · correction policy |
| 2 | the nine refusals · how to file a records request |
| 3 | first "we asked" post |
| 4–7 | Dublin, one document per post, twice a week |
| 8 | Episode 01, the full cut |
| 9+ | alternate "we asked" and "check it yourself", twice a week |
| ~12 | your case, if and only if counsel has cleared it |

Slow is the strategy, not a compromise on it. The account that is still posting
sourced documents in month six is more credible than any single story it could
have led with in week one.
